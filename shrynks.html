Shrynks

Designer, systems & balance · 2026

A modern punk RPG about psychics, built on a dice pool I could test instead of guess at.

  • Systems design
  • Probability modeling
  • Python
  • Worldbuilding

The premise

A psychic cataclysm cracked the world and shrynks emerged from it. What caused the cataclysm is deliberately undefined — the rules present competing in-world theories and never confirm one, because finding out can be the goal of an entire campaign.

Shrynks are persona non grata: imprisoned, exploited, and hunted by a worldwide theocratic order. Three powers hold the map — St. Iver, a church-city spanning what was the American eastern seaboard; Drexlin, the bureaucratic and surveillance heart of global society; and a liberated Oceania that shelters shrynks and has frontier problems of its own.

The tone runs from gritty and paranoid to pulpy, but everything passes through a cerebral, slightly-wrong lens. Even at its loudest it should feel like reality has a seam in it.

The design problem

The theme is identity and control: what your power does to your sense of self, and who you become when you use it. So the mechanics had to make power growth and identity loss the same axis — push harder, get stronger and stranger, slide toward becoming a Psychic Aberration. Pressure builds, erupts as Fallout, and drags you a little further from yourself.

That constraint rules out a lot. Any subsystem that lets a player get more powerful without paying in self is off the table, which is a useful thing to know before writing a single power.

Thirteen Strains — Telepathy, Telekinesis, Precog, Firestarter, Psychometry, Moulder, Blank, Projector, Echoes, Slink, Guidance, Charade, Elevation — each key to an Attribute plus a Skill rather than to a spell list, so a shrynk's flavor of power is expressed through what they were already good at.

The dice, and testing them

The resolution rule

Roll a pool of d6s against a difficulty class.

  • 6 — a Surge
  • 4–5 — a Hit
  • 1–3 — nothing

Every pair of Surges converts into a crit worth 4 toward your score; an odd Surge left over counts as 1. Score = Hits + Surge value. Meet the DC and you succeed; succeed with two or more Surges and it's a Surge Success.

The pair-conversion is the interesting part and the risky part. It means the curve isn't linear — a pool that rolls hot doesn't just succeed, it leaps. Which is exactly the feel I wanted for psychic power, and exactly the kind of rule that quietly breaks a difficulty scale if nobody checks.

So I wrote a simulator: a Monte Carlo run over every difficulty and pool size in the design range, a million trials each, printing a success-rate grid.

Two things fell out of it. The first is a usable difficulty scale. A five-die pool — a plausible starting character — clears DC 3 about half the time (53.5%), drops to roughly one attempt in six at DC 5 (17.6%), and is effectively walled off by DC 7 (1.9%). That gives me somewhere real to anchor "routine," "hard," and "don't," instead of picking numbers because they sounded right.

The second thing was a hole

At every even pool size, one score is unreachable. A pool of six can produce a 12 — six Surges, three crits — and it can produce a 10, but it can never produce an 11. The conversion jumps over it. Same gap at four dice (no 7) and at eight (no 15).

It only bites at the very top of a pool's range, where the odds are already vanishing, so it may not be worth fixing. But it's the kind of thing that should be a decision rather than a surprise, and I wouldn't have found it by reading the rule.
